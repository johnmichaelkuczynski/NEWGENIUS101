Question: Does logic require the existence of non-spatiotemporal entities? Give the property-based argument.
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 9
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Causality; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology
Word count: 226

EXACT ANSWER
Yes. Logic requires non-spatiotemporal entities, and the property-based argument shows why.

Start with a rudimentary inference: "Sally and Fred are both intelligent." This licenses the move to "there exists some property P such that Fred is an instance of P and Sally is an instance of P." That inference is indisputably correct. To validate it, "it is necessary to grant the existence of non-spatiotemporal entities."

Why non-spatiotemporal? Because "instances of intelligence are in space-time. Einstein was in space-time; so was Keynes. But intelligence itself is not in space-time." You will never encounter the property of liking to play tennis; you will at most encounter its instances. "No matter how thoroughly one searches the universe, one will never encounter the property of liking to play tennis."

The anti-Platonist retreat—that properties are just aggregates of their instances—fails. Consider triangularity. "Nothing has this property." If we said it doesn't exist, we'd be saying "there exists a property, viz. that of triangularity, such that, because nothing has it, it doesn't exist. Which is absurd."

So properties exist, and they are not in space-time. Since logic traffics in properties—and in concepts, which are themselves platonic entities—logic cannot be done without them. "Concepts are platonic entities; they are not constituents of the spatio-temporal world."

This is not a metaphysical indulgence. It is the minimum ontological cost of validating inferences we all accept.

Question: What is a property, and why is a property not identical with any of its instances?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 12
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Part II; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Russell's Improvements on Frege's Work   Chapter 6; Causality
Word count: 281

EXACT ANSWER
A property is a way things can be—nothing more mysterious than that. As I've put it, "a property is simply a way that things can be. Equivalently, a property is a possibility." To be round is to be one way; to be square is another. When you jog rather than watch television, two possibilities existed and only one was actualized: "two properties, only one of which was instantiated."

Properties can also be understood as conditions. "The property of being a pink elephant is a condition a thing must fulfill in order to be a pink elephant." Mary's condition for marriage—genius, friendly, never sleeps, a billion dollars—is unfulfilled, yet the condition plainly exists. So "properties that aren't instantiated are just unfulfilled conditions."

Now the central point: a property is not identical with its instances. The reason is that properties are modally elastic while their instances are not. "The property of being a pink elephant exists, but is currently uninstantiated. If there were to come into being a single pink elephant, which promptly died, the property wouldn't go out of existence." But if the property were identical with its instances, "it would go out of existence the moment its one instance did."

The same reasoning refutes identifying a property with a scattered object. "Even though that 'scattered object' is always undergoing changes... the property of being a rock does not undergo corresponding changes, which would not be possible if that property were identical with that object."

Hence: "properties are not identical with sets of their instances... properties tend to be modally elastic, whereas sets are modally frozen." A property can generate different instances in different worlds; the set of its actual instances cannot.

Question: Can a non-spatiotemporal entity have causal powers?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 11
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Do We Think in Words?; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8
Word count: 290

EXACT ANSWER
No. A non-spatiotemporal entity cannot have causal powers, and this is not a limitation but a conceptual truth about what causation is.

My position is unambiguous: "Causes are necessarily in space and time. To be a cause is to induce change. Changes and their causes are ipso facto in space-time." To be a cause is to make something happen, and making something happen is a spatiotemporal affair.

This is why I insist that "nothing non-spatiotemporal stands in them"—that is, in nomic relations. Nomic relations "hold only among occupants of the space-time manifold." My pounding the table is nomically connected to the table's breaking. My not destroying Jupiter is not nomically related to anything.

The contrast is instructive. "My pounding the table is an event; it makes things happen; things can be explained in terms of it; it has a place in the tapestry that constitutes the spatiotemporal world. My not destroying Jupiter is nowhere in that tapestry, and therefore isn't fit to stand in nomic relations to anything."

Now apply this to properties, which are my central case. "Properties do indeed lack causal powers." The property of triangularity "is not in space and, therefore, doesn't have a shape and, therefore, isn't an instance of itself." Properties are "ways things can be"—and "a way that something can be is simply a possibility as to how it can be, and such a possibility obviously isn't a constituent of space-time."

Does this make properties explanatory deadwood? No. "Not all explanation is causal explanation. Some explanation is what we call analytic or conceptual." That there are as many even numbers as whole numbers is explained by one-one correspondence, not by causation.

So: non-spatiotemporal entities are real, and they explain. They simply do not cause.

Question: Why does the existence of properties follow from meaningful predication?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 4
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Part II; Causality; Russell's Improvements on Frege's Work   Chapter 6
Word count: 236

EXACT ANSWER
The existence of properties follows from meaningful predication because even the most rudimentary inferences presuppose them. Consider the inference from "John and Bill are both tall" to "there is something that John and Bill have in common." This inference obviously goes through, and it presupposes the existence of a property—namely, tallness—since "something" here is shorthand for "some property."

Attempts to eliminate properties by paraphrasing into statements about their instances fail. The statement "one can have most of the properties of a great businessman without being a great businessman" cannot be translated into a statement that does not presuppose the existence of properties. The paraphrase project is a non-starter.

Nor can properties be identified with their instances. Some have tried to identify the property of being a rock with a "scattered object" consisting of all rocks. But this fails: even though that scattered object is always undergoing changes—say, in the location of its outer boundary—the property of being a rock does not undergo corresponding changes. If the property were identical with that object, this would be impossible.

Analogous reasoning refutes any attempt to identify any property with any spatiotemporal entity. And Plato himself mischaracterized his own theory by assuming that properties must in some way or other "resemble" their own instances.

So the chain is tight: predication requires inference, inference requires shared features, shared features require properties, and properties resist reduction to instances or to spatiotemporal objects.

Kuczynski 05 — PASS
Question: What are propositions?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 20
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Part II; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Causality; Russell's Improvements on Frege's Work   Chapter 6; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 244

EXACT ANSWER
Propositions are sets of properties. That is my core thesis, and everything else follows from it.

"By a 'proposition,' I mean what is meant by an utterance of a sentence." When I point at Smith and say "that man is a scoundrel," the meaning of that utterance is a proposition. So propositions are sentence-meanings, not sentences themselves.

This is why "different sentences (e.g., 'schnee ist weiss' and 'snow is white') can express the same proposition." The proposition is what those sentences have in common.

Propositions are also the objects of our mental attitudes. "Propositions are the things we affirm. They are also the things towards which belief, doubt, fear, and all other attitudes are directed." When Smith believes something, the thing believed is not a mental event—it is a proposition, and it is mind-independent in the sense that others can grasp the very same thing.

I also define them as "non-derivative bearers of truth and falsity." A belief or a sentence is "true" only derivatively, by association with a true proposition. The proposition is what is true in the first-class sense.

Structurally, "propositions consist of discrete parts; it also follows that those discrete parts correspond to discrete parts of the sentences that express them." That is why I call them digital structures.

And crucially, propositions are properties. "Since sets can, in the way just described, be identified with properties, it follows that propositions are properties." They are non-spatiotemporal, but they are not thereby non-property-like.

Kuczynski 06 — PASS
Question: What makes a proposition true?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 20
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Part II; Causality; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 280

EXACT ANSWER
A proposition is true just in case the properties that are members of it are instantiated. That is the whole of it.

I hold that "propositions are sets of properties; and a proposition is true just in case the properties that are members of it are instantiated." Truth is not correspondence, not coherence, not verification. "Roughly, truth is instantiatedness."

Consider the proposition that Tommy is smoking. On my view, this proposition is a set of properties. It is true if and only if each member of that set is instantiated in the world.

The argument runs this way. "For a proposition to be true is for the world to be a certain way." And "for a thing to be a certain way is for it to have a certain property." Therefore, "the world's being a certain way is identical with its having a certain property." Since a proposition's being true is identical with the world's being a certain way, "propositions must be identified with properties and a proposition's being true must be identified with its being instantiated."

So: "Propositions are properties and truth is instantiatedness."

This is not a human construction. "The world was a certain way before we were around; it will be a certain way after we're gone." It follows that "independently of our having any beliefs or, indeed, our doing or thinking anything, certain propositions are true." Sentences are our artifacts; propositions are not.

A contingent proposition "is true in virtue of some fact about the basic constituents of the world." The truth of "Plato punched Socrates" supervenes on "facts about atomic movements, electron jumps, and mass-energy displacements." Truth bottoms out in the rippling of the quantum.

Question: Can truth be reduced to correspondence between a proposition and a fact?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 4

EXACT ANSWER
Truth is correspondence, but not between a proposition and a fact—that formulation conflates two distinct things. "For a proposition to be true is for it to fit the facts." That is the obvious answer, though imprecise. The precision comes when we see that propositions and sentences are not the same kind of thing.

A sentence is a linguistic entity. A proposition is what a sentence encodes. So "for a sentence to be 'true' is for it to encode a true proposition." But "this isn't what it is for a proposition to be true, since propositions don't encode anything." The proposition doesn't point at a fact the way a sentence does. The proposition just is true or false—it fits the facts or it doesn't.

This matters because the term "statement" is ambiguous; "it has three distinct meanings." Sometimes it refers to propositions, sometimes to the sentences used to affirm them, sometimes to the act of using a sentence to affirm a proposition. Collapse these and you get Wittgenstein's error: "Wittgenstein didn't countenance the existence of propositions, and this obviously had a hand in his erroneously believing that philosophy is the analysis of sentences, when the truth is that philosophy is the analysis of propositions."

So correspondence is the right model, but the relata must be kept straight. Propositions correspond to facts. Sentences encode propositions. Acts of affirmation use sentences. Only the first relation is truth.

Question: Why are propositions not sentences or mental events?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 13
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Part II; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Russell's Improvements on Frege's Work   Chapter 6; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Causality; Do We Think in Words?; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means

EXACT ANSWER
Propositions are properties, and a proposition's being true is its being instantiated. That single thesis already tells you why propositions can't be sentences or mental events: sentences are human artifacts, and mental events are psychological conditions, but the truth-bearer itself belongs to neither category.

Take the sentence "the moon is less massive than Earth." It "is a human artifact; it didn't exist until a few centuries ago. But the truth it expresses is in a different category. That truth exists independently of us." The sentence came and went; the truth didn't. So the proposition meant by that sentence cannot be the sentence.

Nor is it a mental event. When Smith believes that the inventor of bifocals is six feet tall, "Smith's having that belief is indeed a psychological condition. But the thing believed is not. The believing is mental; the believed is not." The proposition is what others can grasp too—"it is also something people besides Smith could grasp, even if they don't."

This is why different sentences—"schnee ist weiss" and "snow is white"—can express the same proposition. If propositions were sentences, that would be impossible. And it's why "one needn't know English, or any other given language, to be able to analyze that proposition correctly," while analyzing the sentence does require such knowledge.

Propositions are non-derivative bearers of truth; sentences and beliefs are only derivatively true. That's the whole point: "propositions are never conventionally true."

Question: What is wrong with treating possible worlds as the basis of counterfactual truth?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 20
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?
Word count: 285

EXACT ANSWER
The possible-worlds analysis of counterfactuals fails on two fronts: it cannot explain our knowledge of counterfactual truths, and it cannot even state what counterfactuals mean without collapsing into circularity.

Start with knowledge. "We obviously can’t see or otherwise sense-perceive what is going on in other worlds; nor can we sense-perceive evidence of what is going on in such worlds." Any knowledge we have of those worlds is parasitic on our knowledge of counterfactual truths. So the analysis runs backwards: "our knowledge of (i) has nothing to do with other worlds." We know "if x hadn't happened, y wouldn't have" because we know some fact in our world, not because we've inspected a world where x didn't happen.

Lewis himself concedes the point. "We don’t know about alternative worlds by taking a tour of them." We construct them by "employing a principle of recombination" — applying modal principles we already hold. But then "the worlds answer to us, not us to them." Our modal knowledge "is the tail that wags the dog," and the extra worlds are "useless" for validating it.

The analysis also can't say what a counterfactual is. If worlds are sets of statements, then "counterfactuals merely register interpropositional relations" — and "until this relation is identified, LA fails to say what counterfactuals are. And once it does identify that relation, it won’t be necessary to talk about other worlds at all."

Worse, the similarity metric collapses. To validate "if Smith had not shot Jones, Jones would have lived," we need a world where Smith doesn't shoot but everything else matches — which requires "trillions upon trillions" of miracles, or a "doctored" world. "A theory that only doctored data supports is a bad theory."

Question: Are laws of nature invariant with respect to every region of space?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 2
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism
Word count: 273

EXACT ANSWER
No. Not all laws of nature need to be spatiotemporally invariant.

The argument is straightforward. Some philosophers of science have taken the term "general principle" to mean "spatiotemporally invariant principle," and they have thus taken the position that the laws of nature must be spatiotemporally invariant. A principle or law is "spatiotemporally invariant" if, given any two regions of space-time, it holds no less for the one than the other.

But that demand cannot be met. Many of the dependence-relations on which scientists depend are undefined, and therefore neither true nor false, for almost all regions of space-time. A relation that is undefined across most of space-time cannot be said to "hold no less" for one region than another. It does not hold at all in those regions — it is simply inapplicable.

So the invariance requirement fails as a criterion for lawhood. Not all scientific laws need to be spatiotemporally invariant, as many dependence-relations are context-specific.

Consider what a law actually is. Laws of nature are not perfect concomitances: for it to be a law of nature that instances of phi bring about instances of psi is not for each and every phi to be followed by an instance of psi. There could be such a law even if no phi ever brought about any psi. For there to be such a law is for instances of phi to be dispositive, for reasons of a relatively non-context-specific nature, of the occurrence of instances of psi.

That is the correct picture. A law is a principled, defeasible generalization — not a universal regularity that must obtain identically in every corner of the cosmos.

Kuczynski 12 — PASS
Question: What distinguishes a law of nature from an accidental regularity?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 7
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 254

EXACT ANSWER
The difference is not statistical frequency. It is grounding in dispositional, non-context-specific reasons.

An accidental regularity supports no counterfactuals. A law does. As I argue, "Each of (a) and (c) supports predictions and counterfactuals; (b) does not do so, and neither does an accidental, albeit place- and time-invariant, generalization."

Consider cyanide-intolerance versus shellfish-intolerance. Both may hold universally. Only one is a law. "The difference between (a) x is human→x is cyanide-intolerant and (b) x is human→x is shellfish-intolerant is not to be understood in statistical terms." The reason is that "it is inherent in what it is to be physiologically human (or otherwise mammalian) that a human being be cyanide intolerant."

So the law is rooted in what it is to have a distinctively human physiology. Shellfish-intolerance is not. "If everyone happened to be shellfish-intolerant, while otherwise being just like a physiologically normal human being, none of these instances of shellfish-intolerance would be rooted in what it is to have a distinctively human physiology." A deviation would be "a singularity only in some emptily statistical sense."

This is why I insist that "laws of nature are not perfect concomitances." A law can hold even if no instance ever occurs. "There could be such a law even if no phi ever brought about any psi." What matters is that "instances of phi [be] dispositive, for reasons of a relatively non-context-specific nature, of the occurrence of instances of psi."

Accidental regularities lack this. They are place- and time-invariant at best, but they carry no dispositional force.

Kuczynski 13 — PASS
Question: Why is causation a relation between events rather than objects?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 11
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 298

EXACT ANSWER
Causation is a relation between events because only events can be causes. Objects are not causes.

The rock is not a cause. The rock didn't cause the window to break. What did so was some event involving the rock—the rock's striking the window at a certain time. When people say "Carter was the cause of the recession" or "Hitler was the cause of World War II," these statements are elliptical. They are shorthand for claims about events involving those figures.

This is why I insist that "it is only events that can be causes. Objects are not causes."

The confusion arises because objects have causal properties. But having a causal property is not the same as being a cause. The rock isn't a cause, but it has causal properties. Its having a certain mass is among those properties, meaning that its having that mass is, or can be, a partial cause. Not every property of the rock is a cause—the rock's being such that it either is or is not identical with some number causes nothing.

I also distinguish conditions from events. A condition is a relatively persistent state, like a room's having a certain temperature. Conditions are not events, and so are not causes in the strict sense.

The deeper point is that "dependence-relations don't hold between objects. They hold between events or between statements, depending on whether they're of a causal or a logical nature." Causal dependence is event-to-event. Logical dependence is statement-to-statement. Objects figure in both only derivatively—through the events they participate in or the statements that describe them.

So-called formal and material causes fail for the same reason. They would have to hold between things, not events. That makes the concepts incoherent. Only efficient causality—the relation between events—is a bona fide form of causality.

Question: Can we observe causal connections?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 8
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 260

EXACT ANSWER
Yes. We observe causal connections whenever we observe anything at all. Hume's denial rests on a mistake about what observation involves.

The same point applies to properties. A "steel" object that lacked the requisite degree of malleability or thermal conductivity would ipso facto not be steel. Malleability and conductivity are causal properties. To observe steel is to observe something with causal powers.

So we do not merely infer causation from constant conjunction. We see it. "We see nothing instantaneous." Everything we see persists, and persistence is causal process. And we see things as persisting. Therefore we see them as causal.

Question: Why is persistence itself a causal notion?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 12
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism

EXACT ANSWER
Persistence is causal because to persist just is to be the ongoing effect of prior states. There is no such thing as an object that simply "stays the same" without any causal process underwriting that sameness.

I put it plainly: "Persistence is a causal phenomenon. Spatio-temporal continuity is a form of persistence. Consequently, continuity is a causal phenomenon." And again: "If something persists, there is ipso facto a causal sequence of some kind."

This is why Hume's whole framework collapses. He assumes "there are entirely non-causal descriptions of states of affairs." But that assumption is false. Consider the billiard table: "X's rolling is causal; so for that matter is Y's standing still (relative to the pool-table). The persistence of each of those objects is itself a causal fact."

The stationary ball is not a causally inert datum. Its continued occupancy of that region is itself an effect—the maintenance of its structure against perturbation. "To say that x has endured for a certain period of time, or has moved from A to B, is to make a causal claim."

This has a striking consequence: "no state of affairs can be described by a non-causal proposition, making it impossible even to articulate Hume's puzzle regarding causality in a way that doesn't presuppose its existence."

And it cuts against the anthropomorphic picture of causation as disruption. "From a purely physical standpoint, the transference of motion from X to Y is no more disruptive, no greater a change, than X's rolling or Y's persistence."

So persistence is causal because endurance is not a passive given—it is an ongoing achievement of causal structure.

Kuczynski 17 — PASS
Question: What is wrong with Aristotle's notion of formal causes?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 7
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?
Word count: 272

EXACT ANSWER
The concept of a formal cause is incoherent. It rests on a category mistake about what dependence-relations can hold between.

Aristotle treats formal causes as relations between objects. But dependence-relations don't work that way. "Contrary to what Aristotle thought, dependence-relations don't hold between objects. They hold between events or between statements, depending on whether they're of a causal or a logical nature."

So-called formal causes "don't hold between events (or states of affairs) or statements; they hold between things. Thus, the concept of a formal cause is an incoherent one."

The confusion runs deeper. Aristotle's belief that things have essences is itself incoherent, "and it's a special case of an incoherence that pervades Aristotle's conception of causality."

Consider his own example. "It is essential to a thing's being triangle-shaped that it have three sides. But a given triangle-shaped object's continued existence needn't depend on its continuing to have three sides. Things change shape."

Same with water. "It is essential to a thing's being water that it consist of H2O molecules. But a given puddle's continuing to exist needn't depend on its consisting of such molecules. Things undergo chemical changes without ceasing to exist."

What Aristotle calls essential dependence is really logical entailment. "To say that x's having three sides is essential to x's being a triangle is to say that 'x is a triangle' entails that 'x has three sides.'"

That's a relation between statements, not between a thing and its form. The formal cause collapses into logic, and the material cause collapses with it—"for the same reason."

Only efficient causality survives. "The concept of efficient causality clearly is a coherent one."

Question: Why does Hume's analysis fail to distinguish causation from epiphenomenal succession?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 11
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?
Word count: 307

EXACT ANSWER
Hume's analysis fails because it cannot distinguish between a genuine causal process and a mere sequence of effects that happen to be conjoined. The reason is structural: his conditions are satisfiable by any two effects of a common cause, provided they stand in the right spatiotemporal relation.

My dog Fido example makes this vivid. "Your dog Fido is an infallible earthquake detector. Whenever an earthquake is about to strike, he senses it, and barks hysterically and runs around in circles." When the earthquake then strikes, "Hume's analysis entails that Fido's antics causes the earthquake to strike." But they obviously don't. "The real cause of the earthquake presumably created some disturbance that Fido detected." So "some one thing was responsible both for Fido's antics and for the earthquake. The two effects fulfilled the conditions described by Hume. For this reason, the sequence they formed mimicked a bona fide causal connection."

This is what I call "epiphenomenal causation." As I put it: "When some one thing E1 has two effects E2 and E3 that appear to be related as cause and effect, respectively, we say that the sequence consisting of E2 and E3 is a 'pseudo-causal process' or, alternately, a case of 'epiphenomenal causation.' Hume's analysis cannot distinguish epiphenomenal causation from actual causation."

The deeper point is that Hume mistakes constant conjunction for the causal mechanism itself. "We know this because any given case of pseudo-causation proves, on inspection, to involve discontinuities not involved in their real counterparts." What actually marks causation is continuity—the transmission of mass-energy through a seamless process—not mere regularity. "It is only with considerable artifice that ME is represented as consisting of some event x and some adjacent event y." Anyone witnessing the real process "knows that he is witnessing an instance of causation... because he knows that he is witnessing an instance of continuity."

Question: What is the relation between causation and explanation?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 3
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Causality; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 290

EXACT ANSWER
Causation is a relation between events, not between objects or things. That is the foundation. And since "to explain is to find causes; to explain an event is to identify its cause," the relation between causation and explanation is one of identity of target: explanation just is causal identification.

But this platitude needs sharpening. Identifying a cause is necessary but not sufficient for explaining an event. My example: a vase breaks at t*. It collided with the ground at t. We want to explain why the vase broke. We must identify the specific mechanisms — the vase's mass, the thinness of its walls, the rate of travel upon impact. "Knowledge of the how is knowledge of the what. How-knowledge is high-resolution what-knowledge."

So explanation is not merely pointing at a cause. It is resolving the cause to the level of mechanism. "If all you know about the cause of Tim's death was that it was brought on by his eating fish, then you don't really know what caused his death."

This is why I reject the deductive-nomological model. DN is viciously circular. Hypotheses concerning laws are "distillations of existing, low-level explanations" — so you cannot use laws to ground the explanations from which they were distilled.

What drives the search for explanation is a single principle: "causal anomalies must be minimized." And the elimination of causal anomalies consists in the elimination of discontinuities. "Everything will be explained when and only when it is known, for any given state of affairs, what caused that state of affairs to obtain."

Explanation and prediction typically consist in identifying program-causes — stable conditions that predetermine specific events. Laws of nature are not perfect concomitances; they are dispositive relations. That is what makes explanation possible.

Question: What is wrong with the language-of-thought hypothesis?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 7
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Do We Think in Words?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Causality

EXACT ANSWER
The language-of-thought hypothesis fails because it cannot account for the fact that we don't think in language at all. My central argument is that "we don't think in language, even though, undeniably, knowledge of language enormously enhances at least some of our cognitive capabilities."

Consider the identity claim at the heart of the hypothesis—that a thought just is a sentence tokened in the head. This is false. "Your thinking Bob is dumb (i.e., unintelligent) is not identical with your thinking 'Bob is dumb.'" The thought and the sentence are simply different things.

The hypothesis also collapses under the fact of translation. "People who speak different languages can have the same thoughts." A monolingual Spanish speaker and a monolingual English speaker both believe 1 + 1 = 2, but they use different sentences. So the thought cannot be identical with any sentence of any language.

There is also a vicious circularity in the innatist version. If thought depends entirely on an innate code, then "one can't know those rules, or anything else for that matter, without understanding those expressions. Therefore it is viciously circular to hold that we think in an innately known code."

The advocate's escape—that we merely "conform" to innate sentences without understanding them—fails. "If a sentence isn't understood by so and so, then from so and so's viewpoint it is not functioning as a sentence." An unread book hurled at a roommate is not functioning as a book. An ununderstood sentence is not functioning as a sentence.

Finally, the hypothesis presupposes that all belief is verbal. But "a given person's beliefs can be expressed in many ways, and many of them don't depend on that person's being able to use words." Visual information, for instance, is "inherently incapable of being fully expressed by sentences of English or of any other natural language."

Question: How does Russell's theory of descriptions improve on Frege's semantics?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 10
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Russell's Improvements on Frege's Work   Chapter 6; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Do We Think in Words?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Part II; Causality
Word count: 266

EXACT ANSWER
Russell's theory of descriptions improves on Frege's semantics because Frege's system cannot survive the hard data of belief ascription. Frege treats "the inventor of bifocals" and "the first postmaster general" as referring terms. But then substitution of co-referring terms should preserve truth-value—and it doesn't. As I put it, "1 may be false if 3 is true and vice versa."

This is fatal. Frege's position is "radically counterintuitive" and "logically and methodologically indefensible." His only response was an epicycle: "Frege was aware of the problem just described, and he added an epicycle to his theory that he hoped would save it. It did not."

Russell's theory of descriptions (TD) solves this cleanly. According to TD, the meanings of the problematic sentences become: "Smith believes that something uniquely invented bifocals, and any such thing is 6 ft tall." The description is not a referring term at all—it is a quantifier.

The payoff is exact. "1 doesn't entail 3, and 1 is compatible with 4. 2 doesn't entail 4 and 2 is compatible with 3." And crucially: "TD is compatible with the hard-data, whereas Frege's theory is not."

The deeper lesson concerns the quantifier structure. As I argue, "we must suppose that *the phi* is a quantifier of the kind just described." Why? Because "the proposition communicated by a sentence of the form *…the phi…* has the concept phi as a constituent." Frege's referential treatment cannot deliver this; Russell's quantificational treatment can.

So the improvement is not cosmetic. Frege multiplies senses to save a broken referentialism. Russell dissolves the problem by denying that descriptions refer in the first place.

Question: Why is a definite description not a singular term?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 14
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Russell's Improvements on Frege's Work   Chapter 6; Do We Think in Words?; Part II; Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 244

EXACT ANSWER
A definite description is not a singular term because it does not refer. "Definite descriptions aren't referring terms. No definite description refers to anything." The meaning of "the inventor of Velcro" is not given by picking out an individual; it is given by what whole sentences containing it mean.

That is the core of my Theory of Descriptions. The essence of the theory is given by two assertions: "definite descriptions don't refer to anything; and in virtue of containing a definite description, a sentence attributes properties to other properties, not to individuals." So "the inventor of Velcro is seven-feet tall" does not attribute being seven-feet tall to a person. It says that the property of being a unique inventor of Velcro is instantiated and that the property of being a seven-feet tall Velcro-inventor is also instantiated.

The decisive argument is truth-conditions. "B and V thus have different truth-conditions." If Bob invented Velcro, "Bob is intelligent" and "the inventor of Velcro is intelligent" still say different things. V would be false in a situation where Bob was intelligent and where some unintelligent person had invented Velcro, but B would be true. Different truth-conditions mean different semantics.

Hence definite descriptions are quantifiers, not singular terms. "The inventor of bifocals snored" is true iff exactly one thing invented bifocals, and any such snored. The description encodes an existence-claim, and "the inventor of bifocals," and by parity of reasoning all other definite descriptions, are quantifiers, not singular terms.

EXACT ANSWER
The sentence asserts a quantified generalization, not a subject-predicate claim about a particular object. According to my Theory of Descriptions, "the king of France is bald" has for its meaning a quantified generalization that is either identical or equivalent with: something x is uniquely a king of France; moreover, x is bald.

That is the whole analysis. The definite article carries existential and uniqueness commitments that a surface grammar hides. The sentence means: there exists an x such that x is king of France, and for all y, if y is king of France then y = x, and x is bald.

This dissolves the puzzle that troubled Meinong. We need not posit a subsistent bald king. The proposition is false—not because a nonexistent object lacks hair, but because the existential conjunct fails. "The king of France is bald" means a quantified generalization according to Russell's Theory of Descriptions, and that generalization is simply untrue.

The lesson generalizes. Ordinary language disguises logical form, and philosophy's task is to expose it. When we say "the present King of France," we are not naming; we are quantifying. Once we see this, the apparent reference to a nonexistent entity evaporates.

The same discipline applies elsewhere. It is by examining the causal and logical structure of our world, not other worlds, that we know whether or not a given counterfactual is true. There is no reason, apart from Lewis's analysis, to believe that there are other worlds. And there is no reason at all to posit their existence or therefore to accept Lewis's analysis.

Surface grammar misleads in both cases. Definite descriptions and counterfactuals alike demand paraphrase into their genuine logical form before we ask what they assert.

Question: Why can grammatical form conceal logical form?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 20
Representative quotation count: 20

Word count: 287

EXACT ANSWER
Grammatical form conceals logical form because certain expressions—quantifiers—cannot be defined denotatively, and it is precisely these that force a divergence between what a sentence appears to be about and what it actually says.

Take "whales are mammals." Its grammar makes it look as though it predicates something of whales. But as I argue, "WM isn't about whales." It says that "the property of being a whale has the property of being instantiated only by mammals." The grammatical subject misleads; the logical subject is a property.

The mechanism is the quantifier. "Given any statement of English, or any other non-artificial language, that contains quantifiers, the logical and grammatical forms of that sentence diverge." A quantifier is "an expression having the property that, if a sentence contains it, that sentence is ipso facto to the effect that the extension of one property has a certain degree of overlap... with the extension of some other property."

So the concealment is systematic. "In virtue of a containing a quantifier, a sentence about properties, not about specific objects." Grammar tracks surface subjects; logic tracks property-overlap.

Contrast this with denotative expressions. Where an expression "can be defined denotatively," then "the logical form of ‹E has psi› is: x has psi," and "to the extent that the logical form a sentence containing E is determined by its containing that expression, that sentence's logical and grammatical forms coincide." No divergence there.

The lesson is Frege's: "grammatical and logical form diverge," and grasping this is what "made it possible for Frege to revolutionize logic." Grammar is a poor guide to what a sentence entails. Only logical form—what is "represented by a sentence S* that makes it clear what S entails and what entails S"—tells the truth.

Question: What is a desire?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 3
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Causality; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?
Word count: 355

EXACT ANSWER
A desire is a belief about what will bring gratification.

I want to eat that piece of cake. If I believed that cake to be made of chalk-dust or mud, I wouldn't want to eat it. The reason: I'd believe that eating it would not be a source of gratification. By the same token, since I believe that eating that piece of cake would be exquisitely pleasurable, I do want to eat it. My desire to eat that piece of cake, I therefore submit, is identical with a judgment on my part: a judgment to the effect that eating it would be pleasurable.

The same structure governs aversion. I don't currently want to drink the contents of that bottle of dishwashing detergent. The reason: I believe that it would bring me no joy and much distress. But I would want to drink from that bottle if I thought it to contain a non-addictive, sideeffect-free elixir that induced a state of euphoria. Conclusion: My desire to abstain from consuming the contents of that bottle is a judgment: a judgment to the effect that failing to so abstain would bring me much grief.

This has a crucial consequence. "One doesn't have to have a belief to be in an unpleasant state. But one does have to have a belief if one is to have a desire to alleviate or otherwise respond to that state." A creature can suffer without representing anything. It cannot want without representing what would relieve the suffering.

So desire is not a raw push or pull. It is propositional through and through. "A desire is a belief as to how to achieve gratification of a certain kind." And because desires are beliefs, they can be organized, ranked, and revised. "Smith's belief as to how his desires ought to be organized is itself a desire: a desire about desires."

This is why "implicit thought" is a pseudo-category. A shrew that hides behind a rock has no desire in my sense, because it cannot represent the conditional structure—"What I want is to survive. I won't survive if I fight that animal"—that a genuine desire requires.

Word count: 270

EXACT ANSWER
The empiricist identifies intention with desire. Davidson holds "that what one intends to do in a given context C is identical with what one most wants to do in C." That is false.

Consider the robbery. A man puts a gun in my face: "Your money or your life." Do I want to give him my money? The empiricist says yes—since I want to live, and giving the money is the means. But "I want to live. But that doesn't mean I want to do all the things that are involved in staying alive."

Someone may need a leg amputated to survive. "But they don't want to lose their leg. What they want is to live." They judge that "if they are to gratify their desire to live, they must forego gratification of another, weaker desire." So too here: "I don't want to give the gunman my money."

My intention to hand over the money "is not a desire. It is a judgment." Specifically, "a judgment that I'll be better off if I give the gunman my money than if I don't."

Contrast genuine ambivalence. If I love my mother but she wrote a negative review of my book, "I also want to not give her a birthday present." That is two competing desires. The robbery is different: "I don't have a desire to give him my money. I see giving him my money as a grim concession."

Empiricists also misread higher-order desires. Frankfurt treats self-control as an HOD dictating behavior. But "my behavior isn't desire-driven in any sense; it's judgement-driven." Hence: "Many a so-called HOD is really a judgment."

Question: Can a desire remain present when it is outweighed by another desire?

Yes. A desire does not vanish simply because it is outweighed. This is a point I have pressed repeatedly, because conflating "outweighed" with "absent" corrupts our entire picture of motivation.

Consider the armed robbery. "I don't want to give the gunman my money. I judge that, given the circumstances, if I am to gratify a relatively strong desire (my desire to live) I must forego gratification of a relatively weak one (my desire to hold on to my money)." But this is not the same as wanting to hand over the money. The desire to keep it persists; it is simply defeated.

The proof is behavioral. "If a stroke of good fortune were to intervene (if, for example, the gunman were to drop dead of a heart-attack), I couldn't be happier." If the desire had genuinely disappeared, I would be indifferent to that outcome. I am not. "If, while being robbed, I didn't want to not give the man my money, I wouldn't respond this way."

The objection will come: surely one can want both to do and to refrain from X. Yes—but that is ambivalence, not the phenomenon at issue. "Desires don't cease to exist when they are outweighed."

This matters for how I model the will. My desire to be a professor outweighs my desire for money, and is exclusive of it—yet "If somebody showed up with a briefcase full of money, I couldn't be more delighted." The weaker desire remains live, ready to be gratified the moment the stronger one no longer blocks it.

The same structure governs obligation. When I steal from Smith to save my children, "the obligation not to steal did not cease to exist. It was merely outweighed." Remorse is fitting precisely because the defeated element survives.

Question: How are values related to agency?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 20
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Causality; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 324

EXACT ANSWER
Values are not merely things agents happen to have. They are what makes agency possible.

My view is that "to value something, I propose, is to see it as conducive to the consolidation or enlargement of one's agency." This is the core of my position. Value and agency are not two separate phenomena that occasionally interact. They are internally related.

The mechanism runs like this. "When personal thought becomes norm-driven, it ipso facto becomes value-driven. Norms are standards; they are ways things ought to be. To value something is to see it as norm-compliant – as being an instance of how things should be." So valuing is not optional for a subject. It is constitutive of subjecthood.

This is why I hold that "it is inherent in the concept of action that it be value-driven, and we'll also see that value-drivenness is of the essence of subjecthood." An act driven by a wish to make reality conform to a value is what action is. Without value, there is only reflexive reaction.

There is a foundational value beneath all others. "That norm is given by the proposition that what we should value is the extension and consolidation of agency. This particular value is the ur-value - the value underlying all values." Everything else we value, we value because it fortifies agency.

This is why "the one thing that has intrinsic moral value is the consolidation and expansion of one's own agency, socio-ethical goodness being but an instrumental form of goodness."

And it is why "value-drivenness is the glue that makes a multiplicity of otherwise disconnected mental states constitute a single subject." Without it, there is no self. Only a sequence of states attached to a body.

So the relation is not contingent. "The essence of subjecthood is sensitivity to value and, therefore, to a kind of proto-morality, if not a bona fide morality." Agency just is the capacity to be moved by values one has.

Question: How is repression possible?

EXACT ANSWER
Repression is possible because the mind is not a perfectly unified entity. As I argue, "The mind is not a perfectly unified entity; it consists of various parts that communicate with each other only very imperfectly. The right hand doesn't always know what the left is doing."

This architectural fact is the first of three that make repression possible. The second is that "There are two very different ways in which one can be aware of a given thing"—knowledge by acquaintance versus knowledge by description. The third is that "There are two very different senses in which a given mental content can be 'conscious.'" A content can be a constituent of consciousness, or it can be something of which one is conscious.

Now the crucial move. The conventional conception of repression is incoherent. It says "the person in question has some propositional attitude P such that (a) he knowingly chooses to cease to be aware of it... and such that (b) P is constantly struggling to break into consciousness." That is paradoxical: "to keep P out of his consciousness, the patient must know what P is... But if he knows what it is he's trying to keep out, then he's conscious of it and it is ipso facto unrepressed."

My solution distinguishes repression1 from repression2. Repression1 "is a failure to do something; it is a failure to perform the operations by which one customarily generates one's beliefs; it isn't an operation performed on existing beliefs." It "involves choosing not to learn something that, if learned and therefore known, one would wish that one had never learned."

Repression2 works differently. During what I call FM, "you learned that (2) is the case, and you consciously choose to retain that knowledge. This enables you to have your cake and eat it too." You accept a general truth while refusing the specific, uglier one. "By virtue of knowing (2), you enable yourself to continue to be aware of an ugly and undeniable fact."

So repression is possible because the mind's disunity, its two modes of awareness, and its two senses of consciousness together permit a subject to keep something at bay without ever having to identify it as the thing being kept at bay.

Question: What is the difference between having a belief and knowing that one has it?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 1
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Part II; Causality
Word count: 267

EXACT ANSWER
Having a belief and knowing that one has it are two distinct things. "One must distinguish having a given propositional attitude (or, more generally, mental content) from knowing that one has it."

The first is simply being in a state. The second requires a further act. "Knowing that one feels that way involves making a judgment about one's mental state."

This distinction matters because it explains how self-deception is even possible. If having an attitude and knowing you have it were the same, there would be no gap for deception to exploit. But there is a gap.

Consider what actually appears in consciousness. "Propositional attitudes never make an appearance in consciousness in propria persona---or, rather, they do so, but only in the indirect sense in which rocks and trees do." What is present is "at best some representative or derivative of a propositional attitude."

So on Monday, Smith feels a surge of anger towards his dad. The anger is there. But Smith may not judge that he is angry. He may judge something else entirely.

This is why self-deception is not mere ignorance. "Self-deception seems to presuppose that, at some level, one has knowledge of the thing of which one lacks conscious knowledge."

The architecture permits this. One can feel a certain way "but not know it." The feeling occurs; the judgment about it does not—or the judgment is distorted.

So the difference is this: having a belief is occupying a state. Knowing you have it is making a judgment about that state. The two can come apart. When they do, you have the conditions for self-deception.

Question: Why can rationalization preserve a belief while concealing its real basis?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 9
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Causality
Word count: 304

EXACT ANSWER
Rationalization preserves a belief by severing it from everything that would expose it. The mechanism is decontextualization. As I put it, "the essence of rationalization is decontextualization. The very purpose of rationalizing is to isolate bodies of information that, if allowed to commingle, will lead to an awareness of the very thing one doesn't want to know."

This is why the process requires two distinct operations. "Rationalizing involves two things: (i) Lying to oneself; (ii) Inventing a story to make the lie believable." The lie alone is inert; it needs narrative scaffolding. Hence: "No fraud without a fairy tale." That saying "goes double for the frauds one perpetrates on oneself."

The story works precisely because it is largely true. "Like all rationalizations, RA is largely true. People do need to take breaks. People do need to cool off. But I'm taking those truths out of context." A rationalization succeeds by smuggling genuine facts into a frame that excludes the facts that matter.

And it can only operate where belief is already divided. "One can rationalize only if one has conflicting beliefs. If I knew that RA was spurious, RA wouldn't work." The concealment depends on partial knowledge—enough to be soothed, not enough to be corrected.

The result is a belief held without its real basis. "When a person rationalizes, it is in order to make himself believe that his values validate an act that they do not in fact validate but that he strongly wants to perform." The belief survives because its actual grounds have been quarantined.

Left unchecked, the practice compounds. "Each instance of repression2 creates a hole in one's body of knowledge that one must explain away... the need to plug up these holes disposes one to devalue truth." Rationalization does not merely hide a basis—it erodes the capacity to find one.

Question: Why does self-deception not require a mind divided into separate persons?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 3
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism
Word count: 313

EXACT ANSWER
Self-deception requires only that one is not completely transparent to oneself—not that the mind houses two separate persons. As I argue, "given only that one can fail to recognize some one propositional attitude in two different conscious expressions of it, all that follows is that one is not completely transparent to oneself; it doesn't follow that one can self-deceive; it doesn't follow that there can be some sort of internal conflict---between what one knows and what one wants to know, between what is unconscious (because repressed) and what is conscious (because countenanced)."

That last clause is the crux. The divided-mind picture smuggles in a homunculus—a knowing subject and a deceived subject, each a little person. But the phenomenon doesn't demand that. It demands only a gap between having a mental state and knowing one has it.

So I make the distinction explicit: "one must distinguish having a given propositional attitude (or, more generally, mental content) from knowing that one has it." And crucially, "setting aside anything that has to do with repression, one can feel a certain way towards so and so but not know it." Knowing that one feels that way "involves making a judgment about one's mental state."

This is why no second person is needed. Propositional attitudes don't appear in consciousness directly. As I put it, "propositional attitudes never make an appearance in consciousness in propria persona---or, rather, they do so, but only in the indirect sense in which rocks and trees do." What is in consciousness is "at best some representative or derivative of a propositional attitude."

The mechanism is representational, not personal. On Monday, Smith feels a surge of anger toward his dad—but what he encounters is a derivative, not the attitude itself. He can misjudge that derivative. No split subject required.

Self-deception is thus a failure of self-knowledge within a single mind, not a war between two.

Question: What role does anxiety play when repression breaks down?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 2
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Causality; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8
Word count: 339

EXACT ANSWER
The anxiety that erupts when repression breaks down is not the surfacing of some previously hidden knowledge. It is the affective consequence of one's own thought-processes doing exactly what they were built to do—following inferential trails—despite one's efforts to stop them.

"The anxiety that the subject feels when the forces of repression1 break down isn't the result of some hitherto unconscious knowledge entering consciousness. It is the result one's own thought- processes, despite one's attempt to stymie them, following up on clues that it had good reason to believe would lead to a belief that one doesn't want to have."

This is the crucial point. The subject does not suddenly "learn" the repressed content. Rather, the mind's own machinery—its disposition to reason from available evidence—keeps generating the very judgments one is trying to avoid.

Consider what repression actually accomplishes. It does not destroy the belief; it isolates it. As I argue, "Beliefs can be isolated to prevent them from engaging with other beliefs," and "Repression involves making a belief's conscious representation an innocuous shell." The belief persists, but its conscious face is rendered inert.

So when repression fails, we should not picture a dam bursting with forbidden content. We should picture a stream of activity that was never truly stopped. "Unless one goes so far as to damage one's brain, that stream of activity will eventually start yielding the very judgments that you don't want it to."

Anxiety is the felt signal of this yielding. It is the subject's awareness that its own cognitive processes are converging on a conclusion it has been trying to prevent.

This is why the objection "if you know you don't want the belief, you must already have it" fails. "Suspicion isn't knowledge; and there's nothing paradoxical about not wanting to pursue suspicions that one suspects would, if pursued, lead to knowledge one would rather not have."

Anxiety, then, is the price of a mind that cannot fully silence itself. It is the affective register of an inferential process one cannot help but run.

Question: What does Libet's experiment actually show about free will?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 19
Representative quotation count: 20

Word count: 304

EXACT ANSWER
The Libet experiment shows that you are on the sidelines. By the time you make an appearance, the decision has already been made.

This is not the old determinist claim that your choices are caused. It is far more radical. "It is proof that we what we thought were choices aren't." The conscious events you take to be the very essence of decision-making "are either not involved, peripherally involved, or involved totally differently from how we previously thought."

So what does this mean for free will? Almost nothing, and that is precisely the point. Philosophers keep asking the wrong question. They ask "What does the experiment show about free will?" when they should ask "What does the Libet experiment show?" The first question forces the data into a pre-existing agenda. As I put it, "nothing is gained, and much is lost, by trying to press RLE into the service of a pre-existing philosophical agenda."

Here is the actual finding. "The Libet Experiment is not just an iteration of the very old idea that choices have causes." It shows that "the center of consciousness that a person regards as the nucleus of his very self, and of his agency in particular, isn't."

This is worse than Freud. Freud treated consciousness as "a monarch whose power, though considerable, is limited." But Libet shows that "in the very contexts where this ruler least appears to be a puppet, that is precisely what he is."

Does this disprove free will? No. But it does show that "one's center of consciousness—this being what people intuitively regard as the center of their very selves—has a much more subordinate role in decision-making than people, including philosophers, assume."

Compatibilism cannot absorb this. It assumes your conscious choices are your conscious choices. Libet shows they are "yours to behold, but not to make."

Question: Why does neural activity preceding conscious awareness not disprove agency?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 8
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Do We Think in Words?; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Causality; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism

EXACT ANSWER
The Libet-style finding does not disprove agency because it mislocates where agency operates. What the data show is a timing fact about consciousness, not a verdict on whether decisions are made.

My reading of the experiment is precise: "The subject is conscious of his intention only after it has long been in the making." That is a claim about when awareness arrives, not about whether a decision occurs. The subject "described his decision as utterly 'free' despite the brain activity preceding conscious awareness"—and that description is not simply an illusion to be dissolved.

Here is the mechanism I propose. Consciousness pools two datasets—one about the external world, one about "the internal world"—and "decisions as to how to maximize the subject's welfare are made on that basis." But the presumption that consciousness itself makes those decisions "is not the case, at least not if the results of the Libet experiment are to be trusted."

So what does consciousness do? It "either approve[s] or disapprove[s] of decisions already made." And I go further: it "also actively approves of them, if only after the fact, with the qualification that these conscious acts of decision-approval are experiences as conscious acts of decision-making."

That qualification is the crux. These acts "are decisions—decisions to green-light an existing intention." A veto is still an exercise of agency. Approval is still an exercise of agency.

Why the phenomenology of authorship? Because if these acts "were experienced as exactly what they are—if they were experienced as being cases of approving an intention made by some other party—that might overburden consciousness." One makes "innumerably many choices within the space of a single minute."

The finding therefore warrants revision of "many conceits about the mind—specifically about agency and the role therein of the 'self'." But revision is not elimination. Agency survives as the power to green-light or refuse.

Question: What is wrong with identifying a decision with awareness of deciding?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 6
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism; Causality; Do We Think in Words?; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 265

EXACT ANSWER
The identification is false, and the Libet data prove it. What you experience as your decision is not your decision.

The reasoning is straightforward. "RP is obviously the proximal psychological cause of the action. An intention is the proximal psychological cause of the action. Therefore, RP is the intention; therefore, W is not the intention; it is an awareness of the intention." W—the conscious event you take to be your act of deciding—is downstream of the real intention. It cannot be that intention.

This is not the old claim that choices have causes. "It is proof that we what we thought were choices aren't." The conscious event is "on the sidelines, at least vis-à-vis that particular decision." It is "merely an awareness of that decision, and that decision was therefore made by some other party."

So the identification fails on two counts. First, temporal and causal: by the time W occurs, "RP is almost complete," so W cannot play the decisive role intentions are supposed to play. Second, functional: if we insisted W were the intention, "we would be taking the incoherent view that intentions did not have a decisive role in the genesis of the corresponding actions."

What consciousness actually does is "either approve or disapprove of decisions already made"—and even that approval is "experienced as conscious acts of decision-making." The experience is systematically misleading about its own nature.

This is more radical than Freud. He treated the conscious authority as "a central authority, albeit a weak one—like a ruler who, though weak, is not a puppet." Libet shows the ruler is a puppet.

Question: How does unconscious preparation relate to a conscious action?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 1
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Do We Think in Words?; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Berkeley and Contemporary Anti foundationalism
Word count: 236

EXACT ANSWER
The unconscious is not a backstage area where a conscious decision is later made. It is the place where the action is already underway.

"What we believe to be choices that are initiated in consciousness are not initiated in consciousness; they are initiated prior to any conscious awareness of them and also far outside any such awareness." This is the core of my position. The conscious act is the tail end of a process, not its origin.

The architecture that makes this possible is the barrier between the conscious and unconscious minds. When that barrier is "too rigid," it "prevents the conscious mind from drawing on the power in the unconscious mind." When it is relaxed, the conscious mind "can draw on it."

This is why I treat mind-cure and religious conversion as structurally identical. "Mind-cure has effects similar to religious conversion," and both work "by relaxing the barrier between the unconscious and unconscious minds." The conscious experience of deciding, converting, or being healed is the surface event. The preparation happened below.

The religious person "hasn't discovered God so much as he has projected him." The projection originates in the unconscious and only surfaces as conscious sentiment "when the barrier between consciousness and unconsciousness is relaxed."

So the relation is this: unconscious preparation is the cause; conscious action is the effect that mistakes itself for the cause. The conscious mind is a receiver, not an initiator.

EXACT ANSWER
AA works because it does what willpower and self-knowledge cannot: it engineers a psychic transformation through surrender to a projected superego.

The alcoholic's will is shattered. As I put it, "The alcoholic's will has been completely shattered. He is internally divided: one part of him wants to drink; the other wants to live." The Steps then operationalize this. "The Steps are operationalized through constant action, as opposed to mere intellectual acceptance." The result: "the alcoholic becomes more action-oriented and less focused on his emotions and less egocentric generally." He maintains recovery by helping others, since "to avoid relapsing, the alcoholic must help others with their alcoholism."

So AA works not by curing alcoholism but by managing it—through ego-deflation, superego-realignment, and relentless action.

Question: What is the difference between changing oneself and changing one's self?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 2
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; Why Does Alcoholics Anonymous Work?; Part II; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 296

EXACT ANSWER
The difference is absolute. "A person can change himself, but he cannot change his self."

Changing oneself is the alteration of how one is—one's behaviors, habits, beliefs, and modes of expression. Changing one's self is the attempt to alter who one is—one's fundamental identity. The first is possible. The second is not.

My position is that "one's identity is predetermined. Experience doesn't determine who one is; it determines how one is who one is." This is not a metaphor. It is a structural fact about human beings.

The evidence comes from identical twins raised in radically different environments. "One twin would be raised in a mansion, the other in a hovel." One attends an elite boarding school, the other a local public school. Yet "such twins were identical in respect of their IQs, degree of spirituality, and to what degree, and in what manner, they were social or anti-social."

The environment shapes the expression. It does not shape the essence.

This is why "changes to oneself, if they are not to render one's self defunct, must occur within limits set by one's inborn biological structure." You can change how you manifest. You cannot change what you are.

Sally's case illustrates the danger. She attempted to change who she was, not how she was. This is "psychological suicide." She was "trying to change who she is, not how she is." And "one can't change who one is."

The consequence is severe. "Qua SC-believer, she doesn't have the support of her own native endowment." Initially she had it. That is why she could draw on her intellect and determination to carry out her deoperationalization. But "having carried it out, she no longer has it."

Only value-based psychological architectures can undergo sociopathization. The self is not one of them.

Question: Why is psychological identity constrained by inborn biological structure?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 4
Representative quotation count: 20

EXACT ANSWER
Psychological identity is constrained by inborn biological structure because your values are wired into your emotional architecture, and you cannot simply swap them out.

I put it plainly: "certain value systems are internal to his/her emotional architecture." That means the range of value systems you can accept is not infinite. "The range of admissible value systems is limited by immutable facts about our psychology (and biology)."

The mechanism is the same one Chomsky identified for language. "Any humanly learnable language satisfies rather narrowly defined formal constraints," and this shows "that the range of languages that is viable (for humans) is set by innate facts about our psychological-biological structure." Values work the same way. "The range of psychologically (and biologically) admissible values is limited."

So the constraint is not merely practical. It is constitutive. "You cannot change (except within fairly narrow limits) how you emotionally react to situations." And since you can change your value system only by changing your emotional architecture, "you cannot (except in trivial respects) change your value system at all."

This is why I distinguish changing yourself from changing your self. "A person can change himself, but he cannot change his self." The twin studies confirm it: identical twins raised in radically different environments—mansion versus hovel, elite boarding school versus local public school—remained "identical in respect of their IQs, degree of spirituality, and to what degree, and in what manner, they were social or anti-social."

Experience determines how you are who you are. It does not determine who you are. "One's identity is predetermined."

You can comply outwardly with a value system that brings you misery. But "if your emotions reflect your actual values, then you don't really accept a value system that brings you misery." The biology sets the boundary.

Question: Why is the attempt to empiricize psychology not identical with making psychology scientific?
Answer type: direct_aligned
Direct evidence count: 4
Representative quotation count: 20
Named works: Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology   Volume 8; Empiricism and the Foundations of Psychology; Causality; Chapter 27   How Is Rationalization Possible; What Are Propositions and What Is Truth?; Libet’s Experiment   Why It Matters and What It Means; Properties and Non-Spatiotemporal Existence
Word count: 269

EXACT ANSWER
The attempt to empiricize psychology is not identical with making psychology scientific because science is not about sticking to the facts. It is about organizing the facts.

The empiricist's injunction that we "stick to the facts" is either the triviality that correct theories are fact-consistent or it's the absurdity that one can model data without extrapolating. Science, properly understood, is "about positing hidden structures to restore to nature the continuities of which observation strips it."

This is why the empiricization of psychology is not its scientization. Empiricism treats the psychological realm as "a series of disjoint ex nihilo obtrusions into an otherwise integrated, explanatorily well-behaved world." That is not a scientific picture. It is the dissolution of a scientific picture.

The deeper problem is that "introspection" is an act of conceptualization, not theoretically innocent reportage. This fact is prima facie inconsistent with empiricism. Hume simply overlooks it. Husserl tries to reconcile it by stripping introspection of its conceptual component—but since introspection is conception, this approach is doomed to fail.

There is also a categorial confusion at work. Objectively speaking, the facts modeled by psychology are the same as a subset of those modeled by physics. But epistemically speaking, "the one set of facts doesn't even overlap with the other." Psychology and physics don't converge on any one set of facts. What they're studying is not the same.

So the sterility of empiricizing psychology is not incidental. It reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of what science does. To the extent that my points are not misguided, I have helped psychology begin to undo the damage that empiricism has done to it.
