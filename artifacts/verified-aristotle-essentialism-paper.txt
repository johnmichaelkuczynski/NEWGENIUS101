My essentialism locates essence in teleological form-matter composition knowable through philosophical analysis, while Kripke-Putnam essentialism locates essence in microphysical constitution discovered empirically and fixed by causal-historical reference chains.

Matter provides the substrate for concrete substances, but form provides the organizing principle: “Therefore these materials are principles and parts of the concrete things, while of the form they are neither parts nor principles.” Form thus constitutes essence as the actualizing principle rather than material composition.

The essence of a thing is expressed through definition capturing the formal cause: “What, then, you are by your very nature is your essence.” This unified principle makes you the kind of being you are.

Essential properties function as demonstrative principles, not conclusions. “We may generalize by supposing that it is possible to prove the essential nature of man.” Attempting such demonstration produces circularity: essence must ground proof rather than emerge from it.

Form possesses ontological priority over matter in my metaphysics. “For the formal nature is of greater importance than the material nature.” This priority manifests in how form determines a substance's capacities and actualizes mere material potentiality.

Form explains causal powers by actualizing potentialities in concrete substances. “A thing is capable of causing motion because it can do this, it is a mover because it actually does it.” The formal nature thus grounds both capacity and its exercise in actual causation.

Species membership requires instantiation of substantial form rather than satisfaction of independently necessary conditions. “Let C be man, A man’s essential nature—two-footed animal, or aught else it may be.” The essence combines genus and differentiae into a unified definition that captures what the substance fundamentally is. “The ‘animal’, then, present in each species of animals will be animal-itself.” This animal nature present across instances constitutes the substantial form defining membership in that natural kind through teleological organization.

Essential differentiae function as an integrated unity, not as isolated traits. “No single differentia, I repeat, either by itself or with its antecedents, can possibly express the essence of a species.” Essence requires the complete structural formula, not any single component considered separately.

When attempting to establish essence, I must avoid circular demonstration. “Thus they who prove the essential nature of soul or man or anything else through reciprocating terms beg the question.” The error lies in using convertible terms that already presuppose the essence under investigation. “Therefore the argument assumes what it has to prove, since B too is the essential nature of man.” Such reasoning fails precisely because the demonstration builds upon the very nature it purports to prove, rendering the argument fundamentally question-begging rather than genuinely demonstrative.

My approach to essence rests on a fundamental epistemological principle: “Demonstrative knowledge must rest on necessary basic truths; for the object of scientific knowledge cannot be other than it is.” These necessary truths provide the foundation for understanding essential properties that define natural kinds.

Generation exemplifies this foundational structure. “The female, then, provides matter, the male the principle of motion.” The male principle initiates the developmental process that actualizes form in matter, demonstrating essence as teleological organization rather than static constitution.

The developmental trajectory demonstrates formal priority over material substrate: “For primary cause constitutes the nature of an animal much more than does its matter.” The organizing principle directing development constitutes essence more fundamentally than underlying matter receives it.

Form exhibits priority over its definitional components. “The parts of the formula, into which the formula is divided, are prior to it, either all or some of them.” This priority structure reflects how essence unifies multiple elements into substantial actuality rather than mere aggregation.

The production of substance reveals the inseparability of form and matter: “For to make a ‘this’ is to make a ‘this’ out of the substratum in the full sense of the word.” Actualizing form in determinate matter constitutes genuine substance-making, not mere combination of pre-existing elements.

Positing separate Forms for genus and differentia generates problematic multiplication: “‘animal’ and ‘two-footed’, and also ‘man-himself’, will be Forms of man.” The animal and two-footedness in man must be understood as aspects of one substantial form, not independent entities.

Natural explanation requires recognizing two distinct causal principles: “There are then two causes, namely, necessity and the final end.” Material constraints determine what developments remain possible, while teleological causation explains why actualization proceeds toward determinate forms.

My metaphysical investigation targets being in its most fundamental character: “Therefore let these be dismissed, and let us consider the causes and the principles of being itself, qua being.” This inquiry examines what makes anything be at all, not merely what makes particular things change.

My approach to essence differs fundamentally from what Kripke and Putnam propose. I ground essence in teleological structure and functional organization. The essence of an eye is to see, the essence of a heart is to pump blood. Form actualizes matter toward characteristic activities and ends.

Kripke and Putnam reject this teleological framework entirely. For them, essence resides in microstructure—the molecular composition that determines kind membership. Water is essentially H2O regardless of its function or purpose. The internal constitution fixes natural kind boundaries through causal-historical chains of reference.

I have established the fundamental architecture of natural substance: “But that the principles are three, and in what sense, and the way in which each is a principle, is clear.” This threefold structure—form, matter, and privation—grounds essence in metaphysical composition, not semantic stipulation.

My science stands apart from all others in dignity, though not in practical necessity: “All the sciences, indeed, are more necessary than this, but none is better.” This superiority reflects philosophy's grasp of ultimate causes rather than mere empirical facts.

Thus my essentialist doctrine locates necessity in the unfolding of natural potentialities toward their proper actualization, while the modern account reduces essence to static microstructural composition devoid of intrinsic purpose. Where they seek merely material constituents and rigid designation, I discern the formal causes that genuinely explain why substances possess their determinate natures and characteristic operations, thereby revealing the intelligible structure that pervades all natural being.
